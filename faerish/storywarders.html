Clause & phrase syntax

  • Base order is O–S–V; first person uses S–O–V. The attested frames are V+S and S+V, OSV and SOV, IO+DO+S+V and S+IO+DO+V, PP+S+V and S+PP+V. The first-person seat accounts for each latter pair.
  • ghiæan is the become-copula: Duílúch Fæ'du'ærs fæ ghiæan. Verbs may stand without objects: Theváre fæ. Indirect objects form a separate category and take duîm: Duîm-fær niïl Rîbha fæ gæn. Other arguments may enter through by, mé, or tmesis.
  • Existentials use a donor: Chair í gæn, “rain it gives”; first person has Mith Me'hair'os gæn, “we Rain-ours give”. A wholly dropped copula defaults to the speaker: Threïg, “I’m tired”.
  • Adverbials tend to front. Time, place, and manner may reorder for emphasis. Degree precedes an adverbial, a modifying clause follows it, and tmetic degree wraps it: yail ro'arch'il sót by / ya'ro'arch'il'il sót by.
  • Adjectives take arguments through prepositions, compounds, or tmesis—not direct objects. The common order is argument then adjective: fær uperya e, “proud of fær”. Degree takes Or…il, Ro…il, or 'ya; comparison takes iy; purpose takes by: Or'threïg'il côs by, “not tired enough to sleep”.
  • Adverbs and adverbials take the polarity-and-degree set 'ya, 't/'thte, aoa, or…il, and ro…il. PPs may modify them with by: lus Owis malach by, “the night late through”. Bare measures need no by.
  • Prepositions are preposed. They may stand with an understood argument, take coordinated arguments, receive adverbial modification, or be followed by another PP or a measure: Oob lus Math o Claim o Gloiyornugh; Oob lus Phailé fæ dræao nhœme; Ythús Urgea mêdhië.
  • Noun tails may carry deixis: lus Thrugh ios thurn, “the train there”. Multiple modifiers order by relevance; English-style order breaks a tie. In tmesis the leftmost modifier is outermost: re'bi'o'car'ld'g'd, “red big old car”.
  • lus, niïl, and possessives share one article slot. In existentials a possessive fills it: Mis Scale mith gæn, “there is a car”. Numerals count directly with the plural noun; quantifiers ordinarily use partitive e.
  • Heavy material does not shift right. It moves into a fresh Aich— clause or remains packed inside a relative: Lus Hín nhœme. Aich— mé-e fær éx Nímhogê eibh / Lus Hín ythénÿ éx Nímhogê mé-e eibh nhœme.

Coordination

  • o and · œr or · ax but · æxó and/or · aoæx either-or-neither · aoœr neither-nor. œr and ax appear between every pair; o, æxó, and aoæx may instead precede only the last item.
  • Initial O opens a list joined by o, œr, or ax: O Cafi œr Sî offers coffee or tea while leaving both present. It does not open an æxó or aoæx list.
  • The family joins clauses, NPs, adjective phrases, verbs, and adverbials. The conjuncts need the same function, not the same surface form. Where the syntax permits it, a conjunct may attach to an earlier layer rather than only its immediate neighbour.
  • A repeated be may disappear from a later conjunct. Within coordinated NPs, adjective phrases, and adverbials, identity permits either a gap or stand-in uain. Echo-repetition remains question-work, not coordination-work.
  • Each conjunct keeps its own polarity; negation does not spread through o. Voice may vary under one shared subject.

Negation

  • Œr 'thte is the complete fragment “Or not”, not a word for nor; aoœr supplies neither-nor.
  • A high negative may cover either the matrix or embedded reading: Ten yiosnÿ fæ nhœmea silgá'thte. Docking it on the embedded verb disambiguates: Ten yiosnÿ fæ nhœmea'thte silgá.
  • Double negation attenuates rather than reinforces. “Not theren’t” gives “thereabouts”; aoa niri'thte, literally “not justn’t”, gives “just about”.

Anaphora & reflexivity

  • Where be marks person or number, the subject may drop; the licence is optional. With be wholly absent, the predicate is first person by default. Second and third person ordinarily retain at least the copula.
  • fæ is the universal person pronoun; í handles things and cía plural persons. The Síonæyais broadly reject gender-ranking among pronouns and use fæ regardless of the subject’s preference.
  • Ordinary pronouns may refer across coordination, subordinate boundaries, sibling subordinates, and sentence boundaries. Inside one clause, two occurrences of fæ ordinarily denote two people; coreference uses sê.
  • Text-deixis supplies near/far × back/along/plain: iosiyao farther back · iosaoya farther along · sísiyao nearer back · sísaoya nearer along · iosiya farther · sísiya nearer. “Along” points toward the about-to-be-said.
  • sê is free, invariable, and logophoric: it may reach its reporter across a clause boundary. Naked sê is first-person or generic; compounds include í-sê, sê-fær, miths-sê, and cías-sê. No verbal reflexive affix exists.
  • Reflexives remain available inside nominalisations: Eë'shrai-shrai's sê-fær, “the praising of færself”. In ordinary NPs they need no special picture-noun rule.
  • Plural sê-forms are reciprocal. cías-sê also supplies singular “alone”; with mé it forms the alone-construction, and wil'ya adds reassurance: wil'ya cías-sê mé.

Comparison & degree

  • Comparatives use -mÿa on the compared term and bare iy before the standard: Holdhmÿa iy tens. iy never stands without its standard. If the comparison becomes a fragment, the whole iy-phrase disappears: Holdhmÿa.
  • Repeated material in the standard normally reduces to its NP, although its copula may remain. A clausal standard bears visible y- marking.
  • Predicative comparison leaves iy free. Attributive comparison may fold the head noun into the standard: Niïl holdhmÿa Fæ'hín-iy'ær, “a man taller than fær”.
  • sin…sy forms equality and correlative comparison: sin mordach sy fær, “as big as fær”; sin aernmÿa sy sainmÿa, “the hotter, the better”. Equality and inequality therefore use different frames.
  • mÿel / niyel form superlatives. sinya marks a large degree; Or…il and Ro…il scale degree without a standard. Double-negative attenuation supplies “rather” or “approximately”.
  • The same machinery reaches adverbs: equality by sin…sy, comparison by -mÿa, and extreme degree by a tmetic litotic wrap: Ten í or'mordachië'il—'t craithië fithea, “I ate extremely hungrily”.

Possession

  • Alienability rides the donor: mith gæn is inalienable; í gæn alienable. Compare the two hand-washing examples: Mis Glaoh-Glaoh tes Sáinsu mé teo mith gæn / Lus Glaoh-Glaoh tes Sáinsu mé teo í gæn.
  • Tenure rides the verb: plain gæn leaves tenure unmarked; gænyin is temporary; gilæn eternally regives. The family takes ordinary past -ea and future ië- forms.
  • Unnamed possessors precede the noun. A uniquely named possessum takes the absolute possessive behind its name: faer 26th dormitory beside Dormitory 26-faers. Familiar kin forms likewise include rhié-færs and sídia-teos.
  • NP-internal marking tracks naming, not alienability or tenure; the possessed noun itself remains bare.

Emphasis

  • Stress and melody carry no grammatical emphasis: the Síonæyais may shout and stress as they please. Constituents cleft; polarity takes the clitic kit; verbs repeat after themselves: Fær mæ łathn! Łathn!
  • Sentence-opening clefters stand bare. Mid-sentence relatives wear y…nÿ. A sentence that has hosted a cleft takes the atá family; iostá may close the cleft explicitly when its boundary would otherwise be unclear.
  • NP emphasis uses the resumptive frame: NP first, í or fæ reflecting it, ordinary be last: Rîbha-e John í cía craigea bhie. NP-internal focus moves only with the whole NP.
  • Affirmative emphasis may double across carriers: bháya'ya; fith'ya bha. Negative emphasis chains the single 't: fith't o 't o 't. Several constituents or conjuncts may each take their own polarity stamp.
  • Declarative emphasis does not otherwise reorder the clause. The copy-like colloquial Fith-fith: ten fith, “I am eating”, belongs to first person; its formal counterpart is Ten dús Fith-Fith más.

Topic

  • Topic devices are S'air'n X · O • o e X • · Efui • X: · postposed s'efui-e'in · bare initial Sin X. Their punctuation is part of the form: the Efui topic closes with a colon and the conversational shifter with a bullet.
  • No bare movement makes a topic. The first-person colloquial form is O Scóhn ∆ ten mac, “And John — I like”: one elided o does several jobs and the up-trill supplies the closing boundary.
  • Topics sit parenthetically at major clause seams, initially or internally, never phrase-internally or after final be. The sentence’s closing copula closes over an inserted topic. A later clarification is a fresh pointing fragment, not a shifted constituent.
  • Topic-dislocation wears a marker; emphasis-dislocation is bare. The resumptive is í for things or fæ for persons and may elide beneath the first-person marker: O Te'fhó'rhié's'os ∆ ten laíea.
  • Topics may stack, recur, and enter subordinate clauses. The first subordinate depth is plain; deeper topic paths take bare y-, never nÿ. NP-internal material travels only with the whole NP, and coordinate constituents travel together or not at all.
  • NPs and gerund-NPs topicalise directly. Adjectives and verbs enter through nominalisation; circumstances through a suitable noun. Whole clauses may ride in sin e X. Topic agreement does not exist.
  • Topicalisation is optional. No separate contrastive-topic form or intonational topic-marking is needed; contrast may be folded into the existing tmesis-and-polarity machinery.

Syntactic relations

  • Danæam has no case morphology. Order, prepositions, clitics, and tmesis carry syntactic relations.
  • Indirect object takes duîm, free or folded: duîm-fær / fæ'uîm'ær. Comparison takes iy. Equation takes sin…sy.
  • Subject-complements and object-complements stand bare: Ten Væx farmea eibh; Mith fær Væx farmea. Becoming and appointment instead use fær-dúe plus an -r state noun: Mith fær-dúe Xæxr ghiæanea; Mith fær-dúe Cáithoorhr oothíthea.
  • Passive agents are dræao-sources: Dræao lus Ufín Ooraithr fæ ghiæanea. by remains the material, manner, or means-channel.
  • The ceremonial eternal registers use Ton/Tonu for creative force, þin/þis for anticreative force, and Gil/Aól for gift/entry. Hth'—…—'om swallows a proposition, dash-seams holding its scale; an =-chain links verbless predication. Hthom't is “worldn’t”.
  • Adjective-governed arguments trail in P-phrases or enter compounds and tmesis. férás-ufsae is “boy-crazy”; Férás casually means sexual tops and/or dominants in an agender culture with gendered sub- and minority cultures. aoyth rhié-færs gives “like his father”.
  • The infinitive is bound u- (from du-): usót, “to go”. Its subject takes dúe; goals may prefix similarly: Dúe sê ułiuafth usót. Gerunds reduplicate inside the split eës frame: Eë'shrai-shrai's sê-fær.
  • æœá negates an NP; æœá-e means “without”: Ten æœá-e Fía más. Benefactive and purpose take dúe; recipient takes duîm: Ten dúe Piell í átea / Ten duîm Piell í gæn. Source takes dræao.
  • Circumstance “with” packs into a relative with trailing mé-e and atá: Niïl Hín ythénÿ reinach Sáinsu mé-e atá. Its negative may use aoa atá, atá'thte, or thé + æœá-e + bhie.
  • Quality uses e in either direction or derived -ach: Hín e Gree · lus Gree e lus Hín · Grëach Hín. Quantity uses Q + e + N: Niïl Lund e Shar.
  • Material and means take by: Tïc by Úrsapê. Abstract association retains e; purpose takes dúe. Manner may use by, tmesis, or -ië: by Elin ghuethnea · El'huethnea'in · elinië ghuethnea.
  • Cause has several attested construals: e for origin, gæn for what gives the result, dræao + ghiæan for what it comes from, and duín + tug for the state reached. A passive causal phrase may front with yth.
  • Function extends sin…sy to nouns: Ten sy Cthug sin lus Acusthia lynœa. Reference uses e: Ten fær e Atruens'ya saíea. Essive uses possessed mé plus an -r state noun; translative uses fær-dúe plus an -r state noun.
  • Partitives use e and normally place definiteness inside the complement: ssai e Frælithi'ya. Demonstratives front and pluralise with cías: sícías Atruensê, iocías Atruensê. Neutral 'ya follows the noun.
  • Negative quantity includes niuain, aoa uain, æouain, and ssíns. The existential alternative places æœá on the NP and uses dummy í: æœá Frælithi'ya í gænyin.
  • Price takes dúe; value takes e. Distance and duration are bare measures; extent is N + e + measure. wythil is concessive; metałie includes; a negated y- clause excludes; aich adds.
  • Local relations include In at rest, dúe toward, dræao from, and by past. Time uses In, dúiri until, drúræao since/after, duílín within, duáo ago, and aoíth before. Periodic and future-delay expressions use In; duration uses a bare measure.
  • Definite NPs use a proper name, lus, or 'ya. Proper names take no article and carry possession behind them. niïl is optional; bare plurals and masses may be indefinite, referential, or generic. -ê is the general plural, with irregular plurals such as Frælithi; -r makes a unit from a mass: uain Wícr.
  • Vocatives use ∆ or V; intimacy may use Scéan-teos. Honorific style remains productive: Fær Alcuilaí Sê-dúi'gheo-e'ri. “We firemen” uses mé-apposition: mith Łúgrchainr-mé. Mith ax ten'ya means “we, but especially I”.
  • Nonrestrictive relatives are ythés / ythésnÿ. Citation uses the bare lemma, with nouns capitalised; labels and shop-fronts are bare NP fragments.

Voice & valency

  • The personal passive is -ea participle + be, with the patient as subject: farmea eibh. A direct object and some other constituents passivise; an indirect object does not. The be-grid supplies tense.
  • The passive agent takes dræao; by remains means. Dynamic passive uses the ghiæan frame; static passive uses the ordinary be frame.
  • An impersonal frame uses dummy í, as in the negative existential. It is available with direct-object, other-object, and objectless verbs, but not by promoting an indirect object.
  • Valency may decrease simply by leaving an argument unsaid; object-drop is visible in Theváre fæ. No reflexive or reciprocal verb-form exists: the sê family supplies both.
  • Causatives use fær-dúe + an -r state noun + ghiæan or another causative verb. The causee may be omitted, and the same structure serves intransitive, transitive, and ditransitive bases.

Aspect & mood

  • Bare verbs cover perfective and habitual readings. The continuous uses dús + reduplicated gerund: Ten dús Fith-Fith más. ghiæan is ingressive; the tenure forms supply iteration; thuíl is terminative.
  • The perfect uses stíl- and exists in present, pluperfect, and future perfect: saige · Ten stílsótea · Iëao As í iëátea. The same form covers result, experience, continuation, and recent completion.
  • stíl “still” contrasts with thuíl “no longer”. Aspect forms may combine with tense, voice, mood, and nonfinite forms.
  • Syá marks possibility or contingency. Mee is monitory. The ordinary imperative also carries obligation; O • frames the optative.
  • The first-person self-vow Ai'en'lith is narrativemancy: the speaker stories femself into the deed.
  • Objects, indirect objects, and pronouns may enter complexes by tmesis; subjects do not.

Numerals & quantification

  • Attributive cardinals are unchanged: ssai Lundê, cliff Lyrê, éx Nímhogê. Counted nouns take their plural form; no noun class or gender changes the numeral.
  • Ordinals use the C'…'ed machinery: C'achér'ed, “second drawer”.
  • Quantifier compounds include uain't-aoa “someone”, æouain “none”, and Wiunail/Wilhíæ “everyone”. Reduplication belongs to gerunds, not quantification.
  • mith ssai means “we two”; no dual pronoun is required.

Derivation & word formation

  • -aí forms abstract nouns: Grëachaí, mordachaí, Státachaí, Ærmuchaí. These behave as ordinary nouns. The -ach stage may be skipped when its meaning would be absurd.
  • -æn forms verbs from nouns: elf → elfæn. -ea forms participles/adjectives; -ië forms adverbs; Or…il, Ro…il, and -mÿa derive degree forms; -r forms states and units; -iya forms one-who/that-which; -ê is the general plural.
  • Clauses may become noun-shaped by tmesis: Ië'mal'rugh'ach'nÿ. Hthom can take an entire proposition as its guest.
  • Complex prepositions form by solid weld: wythil, duílín, drúræao, dúiri. Article welds include dús, dúïl, and ythús.

Phonology & interjections

  • Vowels may stand at word edges and in sequence; i+i at a morpheme seam writes ï. Roots are word-shaped; affixes may be bound.
  • -as shows local suffix harmony. No general vowel- or consonant-harmony system is recorded.
  • There is no distinctive length, fixed stress, tone, or grammatical intonation. Questions, focus, and topic are marked segmentally; shouting and contrastive stress remain expressive but grammatically idle.
  • ∆ and V are parallel vocable trills and the recorded ideophones/interjections. af- and fel- are their segmental dictionary counterparts. V is otherwise absent from the alphabet; ∆ also serves vocative and first-person topic-boundary offices.
  • The trills can continue alongside speech to the sentence’s end or the next •. Humans cannot produce them.

Semantic fields

  • Kinship is agender and may be reckoned by blood, partial blood, oath, choosing, fostering, or affiliation. sídia, Rhié, and te-…-'os are central forms.
  • Blood: Sídílë “womb-kin” · Elintïmma “lineage” · Maíælrhië “grandparent” · Sinithaíl “twin” · e mÿel Ældhr/Frælr Sídia-teos “eldest/youngest sídia”.
  • Partial blood: Sídia-leth / Le'sídia'th “half-sídia” · Sídia-afTo'emma'lach “full-blood sibling”.
  • Marriage and oath: Wælainr “spouse” · Rhié-Emma-mé-aoa “parent-in-law” · Sídia-wal “oath-sídia”.
  • Choosing and fostering: Frœli-tóff “chosen-child” · Rhié-tóff “chosen-Rhié” · Gna'frœli'sth “nest-child” · Yfrœli slaí Yonê-e “hundred-nights-child” · Rhié e Bailch “milk-Rhié”.
  • Bonds: W'ain'al “bond-for-always” · Mainyin “bond-for-a-season” · Wa'eo-mé'l-teos “god-sworn” · Ygaol Tïc-mé walea atá “hearth-sworn”.
  • Affiliation: Gaol e bissal-by “court-kin” · Gaolaí ythé e Win-łixach atá “name-kin” · Gaolaí ythé dúy Lawu rihea “dream-kin” · Gaolaí ythé e Fís mainea atá “debt-kin”.
  • Colours: blys black · isfar white · ruth/Ruth red · lhanc green · Guiïn yellow. Compounds include Em'ruth'mæ blood-red · Blys yon night-black · Glar afthæ sky-blue · Lhanc wíc-e water-green · Isfar óghs-e bone-white · Guiïn yáë sun-yellow.

  • Other clusters include dream (Lawu, Lawubœegh, afSaín, lus Yüain s'ráshea'in, Ixixaíyon), gift (the gæn family, Gæsy, Gil), affect (Iíuu!/Ai'ai! and Síhr…), time (the du- family), cooking (Fía, Bailch, Cafi, Sî, linchs), and -aí abstractions.
