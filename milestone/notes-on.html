Éx— …The Blesses-Devils-Magics Registry…

5—

Famously, Faerish is void of the imperative tense, substituting the prophetic register in its place. Not “go there” but “notice: you go there.” In English, oaths substitute for the first-person-imperative. As the English subject cannot command themself except by swearing to it (“I promise to be there”: 1st-Person’s answer to “Be there”) so the Faerish subject can neither command nor swear except by prophesying an oath’s satisfaction.

See then? The reputational gamble is less with sincerity than foresight. Compare: births, deaths, marriages. The Uplander instructs-by-prediction the newborn’s sex, the truth of its parentage, its bonds of nationhood; the Faerie- and Up-lander alike demand the dead remain so; and marriage—. Faerish oaths follow what you, in an essay yet to form, call ‘the arcane potential of stamps on paper’ (E 84,849, Cisness: Part 2). The identification is the stamp’s action, not the subject’s— you will say. Then point to cisness. As a force, cisness ‘presides over’ and ‘possesses’; seats a gallery of unsuspecting persons as entrants to its spectral family. As conceptual geists, then, stamps corral us. To transness, cisness, and what-else, but those stamps hold no less power and must be respected— or feared. So for the Uplings. Originless, fed bloodless to eternity as aliens within our realm, really, none foretold a fourth clade? That their waters should mingle through our own? — Amaldéu

Ghéx— …a Doll’s Universe…

6—

Again I ram- and re-shackle Faerish prophecy into English imperative. Alternative: “Notice how your lips break; and your mouth; how your tongue tears lengthwise. And notice— you’re learning to speak again.” The balance between these —meditative guidance; firm instruction— is unstrikeable in English. In Faerish, meanwhile, what’s unstrikeable is the delineation. “Once there were no masters. And yet the house was built.”

Fit that into Faerish and get this: ‘Once, any toward mastery not-yet still-were. And but the house built is.’

The ‘and but’ (‘o ax’) even equips the imperative-prophetic register such that the sentence contains: ‘You will have built the house you’ve already built.’ In Faerish, then, routinely, commands and feats of obedience are gramatically organised as if always-already taking/taken place. “Don’t save the world” as— “Notice how the world goes unsaved.” — E
