Coda

I’ve written a thesis concerning an idiosyncrasy characteristic of my own demographic’s writing. That the idiosyncrasy is perpetuated here comes as no surprise, though I swear it was not my plan.

False Cows is no longer a full course, but a picnic: instead of a sectioned arc (critical component, exegesis, creative component), the structure is now secret, though for this assessment I am unhooking the red phone and sharing what I can. (Even still, I recommend this be read last or, if needed, in the event of overwhelming disorientation.)

To open, I list the thesis’s critical and narrational arguments alongside hints as to how they interact and reconcile. Next, I summarise, undersigning the many oppositions and extralinearities of False Cows’s narrative by as extensional of the complexities for trans self-expression posed by language and political context. I call this a ‘strategy’ and cite precedents. Precedents include: ergodic literature, metafiction, and fictocriticism.

In all I exhort, amid any disorientation, to hold fast and trust. The hull will weather.

False Cows is spread across four critical arcs called arguments. While drafting it, the manuscript was indexed with colour-coded titles that (to me) indicated whether a title was or was not a ‘front’ for another, secret title. In my version, for example, “the Creature’s Tongue” was accompanied by a title that does not appear in the final work, “My Body’s Another,” parenthesised and printed in blue. After “[Series of letters]” were the blue and bracketed words, “Nature, Yet.” So, the four arguments, in secret and in order, are “Left Hand Realism”, “Nature, Yet,” “My Body’s Another,” and “Mattering.”

The second and third are exegeses. Generally speaking, the exegetic subject is obvious: for example, a section titled “the Dark Materials” begins: “To make an exegesis of “Stamina”…” However, the meta-exegesis in which “the Dark Materials” falls is, in essence, censored. In one case, e.g., I elected to keep an exegesis written prior to reformatting by overwriting its secret name (“Already Yet,” in this case) with micronarrative bursts of prose: e.g. over “the four primary trace-sources for Already Yet,” I have written, “the four primary trace-sources for See us at the Winter Ball! 18 July at the District Villa! This year’s theme is Snowmen’s Heaven…”

Littered in with these are the short stories that comprise the creative component, not always in the expected order (the stories sometimes appear before, sometimes after their relevant exegeses). Next, “Left Hand Realism” argues the existence of a subgenre within magical realism characterised by a reversal of the Realism-Magic dialectic such that Magic is the point of departure and the authorial motion is toward Realism. Though officially (in my secret notes i.e.) this argument opens at the “Key Terms”-section of the thesis, a few threads are buried in the introduction and others tie off in later arguments. The last, “Mattering,” is a fictional-nonfictional meditation on the complexities of meaning-formation and community-formation as simultaneous, mutually dependent projects. The first three are (almost) in order. (There is bleed along the boundaries, a red herring now and now.) The fourth scatters everywhere. Protagonists venture their own analyses, steal mine, impersonate and threaten one another. Theirs – in the struggle to make themselves sympathetic – is the voice of community.

Anyway, all that confessed, the charge of this section is to provide a coda, orientating the reader and reassuring them that, despite its unfamiliarity, False Cows’s structure is sound. So, here are two discrete summaries of False Cows and what False Cows, in that sense, is.

The first: an alternately fictional, critical, and fictocritical analysis of trans authors’ and creators’ contributions to speculative fiction. Questions driving the analysis are: what idiosyncrasy do trans voices bring to the genre (if any), what interest does the genre hold for trans authors (if any), and what value does the idiosyncrasy carry for broader audiences (if any)? In answer, False Cows alternates between critical descriptions and creative demonstrations of that idiosyncrasy while repeatedly demarcating (via critical essays and, in the creative sections, pointed motifs) the field and territory under analysis — i.e. transness and the creative borderlands between the Speculative and Realist. It is in this creative borderlands that trans authors make our mark. Here, our unique intervention shows. Thus (as with the disambiguation of “transness” as a field) the disambiguation of that creative territory becomes urgent. While the text unfolds, these processes of description, demonstration, and demarcation depend on and, by their dependence, produce evidences of a literary and theoretic resource intuitive to trans authorship yet adaptable to the receptions and creativities of wider audiences and authorships.

The second: a fictocritical retelling of the myth of Queen Pasiphaë and the White Bull, delivered as a collection of documents annotated by that queen in the course of a fantastic adventure within a series of byzantine passages, archives, and cityscapes contained to a cow-shaped entity that Daedalus, eventual designer of the Labyrinth, constructed to abet Pasiphaë’s seduction of the white bull and the minotaur’s conception. When the queen opens and enters the entity, the myth’s telling blooms in a whirl of collapsing (albeit un-collapsed) frame narratives, the gravitation/collapsing motion of each essential to the structural integrity of every other. I will explain myself.

The nonlinearity – an unavoidable descriptor – of the text is representative not of transness’s inherent character, but i. of the semantic striations (the grammatic and lexical channels) along which trans personhood must force its expression and ii. of the ideologies and political contexts (queerness, neoliberalism, patriarchy, et al.) that materially restrict our community to an archipelago of ex/enclaves. Though clear communication is the goal, the media through which transness’s phenomenology must be expressed – i.e. not our own media – require complexity. If a vision cannot be expressed in one language, two? Twenty? If, all tongues in concert, the sight remains inexpressible, perhaps a new language? If one’s body is insensible to the parameters of one reality, visit the next. All to say: trace the outline of a shredded-up, denied-to-exist, and thousand-times-crushed demographic constant – whose reality even the grammars of this utterance were designed to conceal – and you meet with complexity. But the complexity is not that demographic constant’s (is not our) fault. Neither – I must emphasise – is this complexity native to transness.

Our exclaves enclosed and bounded by a thousand alien systems, wherever transness emerges to expressiblity – let alone to mutual recognition and community – it does so under circumstances remarkable and remarkably complex. We arrive by fantastic means, telling stories you neither understand nor altogether believe. To take us for either fantastical or perfidious agents is forgivable. (After enough time, most mistake ourselves for that.) But, in truth, many resent the means by which we arrive. Unlike the queer but not trans subject whose temporalities and temporal orders impose themselves on the structure of both this thesis and our lives, the nonlinearities, interstices, and subterranean currents between, below, and enclosing every reality are not transness’s home. We navigate them because we have no choice and, speaking for many, loathe that we are seen as of them. Such is not a call to action or for sympathy, only a detail that goes some way to report on and clarify the manner of relation. It would be wise, now, to situate the complexity of these creative and argumentative strategies within a preexisting literature, i.e., to contextualise them as not simply complex, but ergodic.

More than ergodic, the thesis is also metafictional and fictocritical: specifically, a subtype of fictocritical imagined in my Master’s thesis (Numbers by Paint) as ‘fictoanalytic.’ (More on that in the thesis itself.) In order: ergodic, metafictional, fictocritical. So, to situate ‘cybertextuality’ within an existing trend, electronic literature researcher Espen Aarseth coined the term ‘ergodic literature’ as its near-equivalent, defining cybertexts and works of ergodic literature, both, as “textual machines, with unlimited possibility for variation” (1997, “Introduction”). Aarseth takes the I Ching for a straightforward example (ibid.):

A text such as the I Ching is not meant to be read from beginning to end but entails a very different and highly specialized ritual of perusal, and the text in a multi-user dungeon is without either beginning or end, an endless labyrinthine plateau of textual bliss for the community that builds it. But no matter how hard I try to describe these texts to you, the reader, their essential difference will remain a mystery until they are experienced firsthand.

If a work is both labyrinthine and adaptable to re-versioning/further creativity, it is ergodic. The necessity of such conceits – labyrinths, adaptability, complexity in general – is intuitable in the face of the aforementioned ‘thousand alien systems.’

Because metafictional texts usually contain metanarratives, metafiction is often mistaken to mean ‘works of fiction that call attention to themselves as fiction.’ Yet, the first coinage of the word – by William H Gass – offers it as the novel’s answer to metatheorems or “lingos to converse about lingos” (1970, 25):

There are metatheorems in mathematics and logic, ethics has its linguistic oversoul, everywhere lingos to converse about lingos are being contrived, and the case is no different in the novel. I don’t mean merely those drearily predictable pieces about writers who are writing about what they are writing, but those, like some of the work of Borges, Barth, and Flann O Brien, for example, in which the forms of fiction serve as the material upon which further forms can be imposed. Indeed, many of the so-called antinovels are really metafictions.

Gass names Vladimir Nabokov as another exemplary of this type and yet, in “Mirror, Mirror” (1986), writes: “Nabokov’s novels… seem like those Renaissance designs of flying machines—dreams enclosed in finely drawn lines—which are intended to intrigue, to dazzle, but not to fly”: a complaint that, for Gass, does nothing to disqualify Nabokov’s novels from ‘metafiction’. As such, “forms of fiction [that] serve as the material upon which further forms can be imposed” must not allude to those modes of generativity and machinism characteristic of Aarseth’s cyber- and ergodic texts. (I.e., the distinction matters.) So— False Cows’s narrative argument draws on metafiction to interrogate and account for itself as it collides with and attempts to pass many gates of comprehensibility and expressibility. Thinking again of territories, exclaves, and arrivals, metafiction is the mechanism by which False Cows fills out its customs-record and proves its onward travel.

Regarding fictocriticism, detailed reference is already paid in the text of the thesis (Anna Gibbs is invoked as a key citation), such that the strategy need not be defended here. But, there is some risk that the text of this coda will trouble False Cows’s admittance to fictocriticism as a genre. Does this picture of False Cows as “a textual machine with unlimited possibility for variation” (ergodic literature) square with Gibbs’s description of the fictocritical text as “a precise and local intervention” (2005)? Not really, no. But does that matter? A meaningful contribution warps its field, sends minute pings that recontour and reemphasise the generating canon. Literary styles, like realities, have their soft edges, and as said, the borderlands beyond are where we do our best work.

The four, secret arguments of the thesis bleed into themselves, the fourth cast as litter among the rest. Upon the first three arguments (i.e. the first’s report on this voice’s uniqueness and, in the second and third, the vivisections of its examples), the chittering of the fourth intrudes and needles as the rattled, aphasic voice of trans community. It speaks itself, as I then argue it must, through opposed and nonlinear syntaxes, narratives, modes of transit, to survive and arrive on the other side of history’s language- and selfhood-restricting, nightmare limits. Even though, in taking the shape it does, False Cows has its reasons – its citable precedents – it nonetheless operates outside those same restrictive limits, bringing its in(ter)ventions in from without. (In, i.e., from the creative borderlands between the Speculative and Realist.) All to say: when the narrative and analysis are sliced open and poked through, trust that the aperture is not the artefact; that they reattach elsewhere; that each needle-prick serves the thread of another narrative, another analysis. Trust. The archipelago’s en- and exclaves will stitch and pull together. The outline will clarify. Trust me. There. If, amid the frenzied doldrums, the compass’s spinning, and the hungry, stranded nights, one principle will guide you through, it’s that: Just trust me.
